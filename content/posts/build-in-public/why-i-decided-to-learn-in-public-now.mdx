For years, I kept a private rule about writing publicly.

I could not publish anything until I felt confident about it.

That rule sounds reasonable. Maybe even responsible. But it quietly kept me from writing anything for a very long time, because there was always a reason to wait.

## The waiting game I played with myself

Every time I thought about writing something technical, a checklist appeared in my head.

Did I understand this concept well enough? Could I explain every edge case? Was there something more authoritative already written about this? Would someone more experienced read it and find an error?

I never finished that checklist.

So I fixed bugs privately. I learned things privately. I made mistakes and corrected them with no record of either. And every few months I would think: maybe now I know enough to start writing. Then I would check the list again and find another reason to wait.

The cruel part of this pattern was that the more I learned, the more I saw what I did not know. Experience did not make the checklist shorter. It made it longer.

## The thing that finally shifted

One specific moment changed my thinking about this.

I was looking at a bug I had fixed about six months earlier. The fix was there. A few lines of changed code, committed without much explanation. I had found the problem, fixed it, committed it, moved on.

When I came back to look at it six weeks later, I could not reconstruct my reasoning.

The fix was there. A few lines of changed code. But I had no record of what I had understood at the time, what the actual wrong assumption was, what I had tried before finding the right path. Six weeks later, all I could see was the delta, not the thinking.

That bothered me more than I expected.

Because in that six weeks, I had probably made similar assumptions in other code without noticing. And if I ever hit the same class of problem again, I would have to rediscover the same ground I had already covered.

The learning had happened. But it had evaporated.

## What I thought learning in public meant

Before I started, I had a specific picture in my head of what "learning in public" looked like.

It looked like those developers on Twitter who post perfectly timed threads explaining a concept with a clean diagram, confident assertions, and hundreds of likes. It looked like authoritative content. It looked like teaching.

That picture was what stopped me.

Because I did not feel like I could teach. I felt like I was still figuring things out. And standing up to explain something you are still figuring out felt like inviting criticism you deserved.

What I missed was that the picture was wrong. That kind of content is a small subset of what learning in public actually means.

## What learning in public actually is

Learning in public does not require you to be an expert.

It requires you to be honest.

The most useful thing I can write is not "here is the definitive guide to React state management." The most useful thing I can write is "here is what I misunderstood about React state, here is the bug it caused, here is what I had to do to find it, and here is how my mental model changed."

The first kind of post requires confidence I do not always have.

The second kind of post is available to me right now, every day, because I am always in the middle of learning something.

That distinction is what changed everything.

## The specific decision to start

After the moment with the forgotten bug fix, I made a simple rule change.

Instead of: "I can write about X when I fully understand X."

I switched to: "I should write about X while I am in the middle of understanding X."

The first rule optimizes for the reader's perception of me.

The second rule optimizes for the document I need to write, not the audience I want to impress (including the confusion) while it is still fresh.

There is a window of time where something is genuinely valuable to write about. It opens when you first encounter a problem. It closes when you have moved far enough past it that the confusion feels obvious and the context is gone. That window is usually a matter of days.

I had been waiting until after the window closed.

## What I was actually afraid of

Looking back, the checklist was not really about quality standards.

It was about not wanting to be wrong in public.

There is a version of being wrong that is embarrassing: confidently stating something incorrect and having someone correct you. I wanted to avoid that version.

But there is another version of being wrong that is useful: sharing your current understanding, having someone add context or correction, and updating your thinking. That version is not embarrassing. It is the whole point.

I was so focused on avoiding the first version that I had closed off the second one entirely.

## What changed once I started

I expected starting to feel like a relief. A decision made, a habit begun.

It felt more uncomfortable than that, at least at first.

The first few posts felt exposed. Not because they were wrong. Because they were honest. Most of the time no one responded at all. But writing down what you are confused about makes the confusion visible to yourself in a way that thinking about it does not.

But that visibility was the thing that helped.

When I wrote about a React concept I partially understood, writing the post made the gaps in my understanding obvious. I would start a section and realize I could not explain it clearly enough. That meant I had to go back and understand it better before I could write it.

Writing became a test of comprehension, not a reward for it.

## What I want people to know before they start

If you are waiting until you feel ready, you are waiting for something that will not arrive on its own.

Ready is not a feeling that precedes learning in public. It is a feeling that sometimes comes after you have been doing it for a while, and even then it is not consistent.

The useful question is not "Am I ready?" It is "Is there something I understood today that I will not be able to explain as clearly in a month?"

If yes, that is the thing to write about.

You do not need an audience. You do not need a polished blog. You do not need a theme or a niche.

You need to start before the window closes.

## Final thought

I decided to learn in public now because I finally understood what I had been losing by waiting.

Not reputation. Not credibility.

I was losing the learning itself.

The confused version of understanding is the version that teaches the most. It is the version that captures the actual thought process. And it is the version that disappears the fastest once you move past it.

Writing it down, publicly, imperfectly, while it is still happening, turned out to be more useful than I expected. It is the only way to keep it.

That is the real reason I started.

And if you are on the fence about starting yourself, that reason is the one I would hand to you.
