For nearly three years, I published nothing.

Not because I wasn't learning.

Not because I wasn't building.

Because I thought I wasn't qualified enough to share my work.

That belief stopped me from writing, publishing, and building a reputation through my work. I spent years reading others' work, wishing I could start my own **coding journey** in the open, but always found an excuse to wait.

Then I realized something: The developers I admired weren't successful because they knew more than everyone else. They were successful because they showed their work. They knew that **documenting your learning** in public is the best way to grow.

This post explains why I finally decided to start **learning in public** and how it is already changing the way I grow as a developer.

## Key Takeaways

- Waiting to become an expert delayed my growth.
- Writing publicly improved retention.
- DailyDevPost became my searchable knowledge base.
- Feedback accelerated learning.
- Building in public creates visible proof of work.

---

## Why I Stayed Silent for So Long

For almost three years, I was a passive consumer. I had exactly zero published posts and kept delaying launching my own blog for over 1,000 days. Every day, I read developer blogs on DEV.to, scrolled through engineering breakdowns on Twitter/X, and watched tutorials on system design, keeping my own ideas locked away in local draft folders.

I kept telling myself a lie: *“I’ll start writing once I become an expert in React, Next.js, and system architecture.”* I convinced myself that I needed to master complex frontend topics like designing a large-scale [frontend architecture](/blog/frontend-architecture-guide) before I could publish anything publicly.

I was paralyzed by the fear that a senior developer would look at my code, call it inefficient garbage, and expose me as an impostor on Reddit or Twitter. The threshold for what I considered "expert enough" kept moving. The more I learned, the more I realized I didn't know. So I stayed silent, keeping my notes and bug fixes hidden on my local drive.

> "The biggest mistake I made wasn't writing bad code. It was hiding my work for three years."

---

## What Changed My Mind

The turning point came one night when I spent nearly four hours debugging a Next.js routing issue. After finally solving it, I realized I had already solved a similar issue six months earlier and forgotten everything. That frustration made me start documenting my work.

I realized that waiting for expertise is a trap because the technical landscape moves faster than we can study. If you wait until you know everything, you will wait forever.

More importantly, I noticed that the developers whose work resonated with me weren't writing clean, textbook tutorials. They were sharing their messy, real-world problems like detailing what they learned from [what debugging taught me that tutorials didn't](/blog/what-debugging-taught-me-that-tutorials-didnt).

I didn't need to be an authoritative professor lecturing from a podium. I just needed to be a traveler sharing logs from the field.

When you change your perspective from "teaching" to "documenting what you learned today," the pressure vanishes. If someone points out a mistake in your post, it's not an exposure of your inadequacy, it's a free code review that helps you grow faster.

**Before:**
*   Learn privately
*   Forget lessons
*   Repeat mistakes

**After:**
*   Document publicly
*   Build searchable knowledge
*   Get feedback
*   Improve faster

## Launching DailyDevPost

To put this mindset into action, I decided to build a dedicated space: **DailyDevPost**.

After putting it off for nearly three years, I finally launched the site in late 2025 with a single article. It wasn't perfect. It wasn't polished. But it existed.

I wanted a **frontend developer blog** that felt like my daily workspace, built on a git-first markdown system. I chose Next.js 15, MDX, and Tailwind CSS. Creating this **developer blog** was about more than just showing off code; it was about committing to **developer writing** as a primary way to learn.

The goal was simple: stop keeping my breakthroughs and struggles locked in my head. I wanted to **build in public**, write about my frontend experiments, and document every time I spent hours debugging a silly layout issue or configured Cloudflare routing. Having written about [edge vs origin rendering](/blog/edge-vs-origin-rendering-nextjs-guide) and [cold start optimization](/blog/edge-performance-cold-starts-guide), I wanted to put those ideas to the test in a real project.

## My Biggest Mistakes

Of course, my transition to building in public wasn't smooth. I made some classic developer mistakes:

*   **The Headless CMS Trap:** When I first set up the blog, I spent two entire weekends configuring schemas, webhooks, and third-party dashboard integrations. It felt like maintaining a second application. If I wanted to edit a single typo, I had to open a browser dashboard and wait for a webhook to rebuild the site. Eventually, I deleted the database config and moved everything to git-first MDX files. Now, editing is as simple as a local text edit and a `git push`.
*   **The Netlify Bandwidth Surprise:** I initially hosted the site on Netlify. While the platform is great, I soon realized Netlify has a strict bandwidth limit of 100GB/month on the free tier. If a post went viral, I risked a surprise bill. To eliminate this anxiety, I migrated the hosting to Cloudflare Pages.
*   **Trying to Sound Smart:** In my first few drafts, I used heavy, formal language like "architectural blueprints" and "system-level understanding." It sounded like corporate documentation. I had to consciously force myself to delete those sections and write like I was explaining a bug to a teammate over coffee, choosing clarity over complex phrasing.

## What Happened After Publishing

Moving to this honest, build-in-public approach changed everything. 

While this blog is still growing, the biggest win has been the immediate shift in my workflow:

*   **Writing saved me from hours of re-debugging:** Last month, I published a breakdown of our blog's static build architecture. A couple of weeks later, our build pipeline crashed after a Turborepo refactor, throwing obscure import cycle errors. Instead of spending hours scouring old Slack messages or online threads, I opened my own published post about package boundaries (similar to the issues I solved in my [Turborepo folder structure guide](/blog/turborepo-folder-structure-scalability-guide)), spotted where I had crossed a dependency line, and fixed it in five minutes.
*   **Giscus Feedback:** By embedding Giscus comments powered by GitHub Discussions, other developers can point out typos, cleaner utility patterns, or library updates directly on the page. Even if it's a simple suggestion, it turns solo writing into an active collaborative loop.
*   **Confidence & Authority:** Writing down why I made specific choices (like choosing [Zustand vs Jotai](/blog/zustand-vs-jotai) or choosing [Server Actions vs useEffect fetching](/blog/server-actions-vs-useeffect-fetching)) forced me to understand the underlying code on a deeper level. I stopped copying and pasting solutions and started owning them.

## Why I'm Committing to Learning in Public

Learning in public has been the single best decision of my developer career. It forced me to stop hiding behind tutorials and start building a real, visible history of problem-solving.

Looking ahead, I am fully committing to documenting my journey here. I'll be sharing my SEO experiments, Next.js migrations, and frontend performance tweaks; both the wins and the embarrassing debugging sessions.

---

## Lessons for Other Developers

If you are still waiting for the day you feel like an "expert" before sharing your work, here is my advice:

*   **Write for your future self:** Write each entry assuming you will need to search for it six months from now to fix a production bug. This keeps the tone practical, code-focused, and down-to-earth.
*   **Lower the bar:** You don't need a groundbreaking paradigm to write a post. Share the typo that took 20 minutes to find, the layout alignment bug you solved, or the Tailwind config tweak that saved your build.
*   **Ditch the corporate jargon:** Write like a human. The best build-in-public posts sound like *"I spent 4 hours debugging this issue,"* not *"I engaged in iterative system-level diagnostic analysis."*
*   **Make it low friction:** If posting requires logging into complex dashboards, you will quit. Keep your content files right in your repository next to your code.
