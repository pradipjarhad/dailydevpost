When I first started publishing on DailyDevPost, I got a message from a reader that stuck with me.

> "Is this like a tutorial site? I'm looking for step-by-step guides."

I told them no.

Then they asked: "Is it like a newsletter where you share tips?"

Still no.

"A portfolio blog to show off your projects?"

Also no.

I couldn't give them a clean one-line answer. That bothered me. It made me realize that being unclear about what this is, and what it is not, does a disservice to people who find it. So this post is the honest answer.

## Not a tutorial website

When you search for "how to fix a hydration mismatch in Next.js," you find polished articles with clear steps, numbered sections, and working code you can copy.

That kind of content has real value. I read it all the time.

But that is not what DailyDevPost is.

Here, the writing happens during the learning, not after. That means you will sometimes find:

- A post about a bug where I went in the wrong direction first
- A reflection on a concept that I partially misunderstood at the time of writing
- An experiment that worked, but I am not certain yet why
- A problem I solved but cannot yet fully articulate in clean, transferable steps

A tutorial is written by someone who already knows the answer and is walking you backward from the solution to the starting point.

DailyDevPost is written from the starting point, moving forward.

That is a completely different kind of document.

## Not a best-practices manual

There is a certain category of developer blog that sounds like this:

> "The 10 rules for clean React components."
> "Always do X. Never do Y."

I find those posts useful as references. But I have never been confident enough in my own opinions to write that way.

At my level of experience, most things are conditional. The right answer depends on the codebase, the team, the deadline, the constraints. What works in a startup's frontend might not work in an enterprise legacy codebase. What is correct for a senior developer might be premature complexity for a junior.

So instead of commandments, you get observations.

- This worked in my context. It might not work in yours.
- This tradeoff made sense at the time. It may not make sense later.
- I used to think X was always wrong. Now I think it depends.

That is not weakness. That is honesty about how nuanced real engineering decisions actually are.

## Not a personal brand project

I have seen how personal branding works in developer content. You pick a niche, you repeat your core message, you build a consistent identity around confidence, you project certainty even when you don't feel it.

That pressure turns writing into performance.

When you write to perform, you skip the confused drafts. You cut out the part where you went in the wrong direction. You present the final version of your thinking as if it arrived fully formed. You never admit that you were stumped for three hours on something you later discovered was a one-line fix.

I find that kind of content exhausting to produce. And I suspect it is less useful than it appears, because it shows readers the destination without the journey.

DailyDevPost is written without that filter. The confused version stays. The wrong assumption is documented alongside the correction. The "why did I do it that way" questions get asked publicly, not polished away.

Some posts here are probably wrong. Some will be corrected later. Some are incomplete because the thinking was incomplete. That is honest, not careless.

## Not optimized for algorithms

I do not write with the primary question: "Will this get traffic?"

I write with the primary question: "Does this capture what I actually learned?"

Those two questions produce very different content.

Algorithmically optimized content maximizes for search intent, keyword density, and content freshness. It asks what people are already searching for and gives them that.

Learning-first content asks what was actually worth writing down, even if no one is searching for it.

The result is that some posts here are probably not findable via Google. Some are too specific to my situation to rank. Some are written about things that I needed to process, not things that are currently trending.

That is acceptable. The writing still serves its purpose: it captured thinking at a specific moment.

## Not about being right all the time

Expertise in public-facing content usually looks like confidence. You say a thing with authority, people trust it, they share it.

I have read posts by developers I respect who later changed their minds completely about their stated position. But the original post stays up, confident and unchanged.

I prefer a different approach. Ideas on this blog should feel more like notes than articles, written to think, not written to perform. Some will evolve. A few will be wrong and corrected. That is not a flaw in the publishing approach; it is the point of it.

Learning in public means your learning is visible. Including the corrections.

## What DailyDevPost actually is

If the above is what it is not, here is a more honest description of what it is:

**A developer journal that is also publicly readable.**

Not a journal in the private, therapeutic sense. A journal in the technical sense: a record of what happened, what was tried, what failed, what worked, and what changed in my thinking.

The difference from a private journal is that the act of making it public adds useful constraints. You have to explain things in sentences that make sense to someone other than yourself. That process of explaining is itself a form of thinking. Writing publicly about debugging a React problem forces me to understand it well enough to describe it accurately. That understanding is often better than what I would have had if I had just fixed the bug quietly and moved on.

**A writing practice built around engineering.**

Some developers keep notes. Some use internal wikis. Some tweet their way through problems. Some prefer forums, where conversation matters more than presentation.

The individual posts are not the product. The habit of noticing and writing, even badly, is the product.

**A record that compounds over time.**

The individual posts are not the product. The accumulation is. A blog post about a useEffect mistake I made is interesting on its own. But six months later, when I can look back and see how that thinking evolved, that is where the actual value lies.

## How to read DailyDevPost

If you are looking for tutorials or authoritative best practices, there are better places. Excellent ones, actually. I read them too.

If you are looking for:

- a developer at a real stage of their career, thinking out loud
- honest accounts of things that did and did not work
- the kind of reasoning that does not usually survive the editing process
- a record of someone figuring things out in real time

Then this is exactly the right place.

Read it the way you would read a colleague's notes, not the way you would read documentation.

## A closing note

DailyDevPost is not built to impress.

It is not built to rank.

It is not built to make me look like the developer I wish I was.

It is built to help me write better, think more clearly, and keep learning in a way that leaves evidence.

If that evidence is useful to you, I am glad.

If what you need is a polished tutorial or an authoritative guide, I can point you to better resources for that.

The goal here is honesty, not hype.

And that means being honest about what this blog is, and what it is not.
